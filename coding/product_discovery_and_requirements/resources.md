## Evaluation rule

Adopt a method when it reduces an important uncertainty or improves a consequential decision. Do not adopt it solely because its artifact looks complete. Scale ceremony with risk, irreversibility, regulation, coordination cost, and product lifetime.

## Lifecycle references

The lifecycle used in this collection is a defensible synthesis of established product-discovery, requirements-engineering, architecture, delivery, experimentation, and operations practices. It is not a single canonical process copied from one standard or book. Consequently, its ingredients have strong references, while their exact arrangement and wording are an operating model proposed by this collection. It is  not *the* standard software-product lifecycle.

### Interpretation cautions

Two readings would contradict the cited foundations:

1. **A waterfall reading.** Discovery, definition, and requirements are not completed once and handed downstream. SWEBOK explicitly describes requirements work as complex, tightly coupled activity continuing throughout the product lifecycle, while continuous discovery and dual-track development keep discovery connected to delivery.
2. **Treating every feedback signal as a reason to rewrite the vision.** Most evidence should first challenge the outcome, opportunity, assumption, solution, scope, requirement, or strategy — a **discovery pivot** in Cagan's terms, which the collection adopts. A **vision pivot** is rarer and corresponds to evidence that invalidates the intended future or target need, not merely to a weak feature or failed experiment. Both terms are defined in the [glossary](./glossary.md).

### Closest books

No one book defines the complete lifecycle. Together, these provide the closest coverage:

1. Teresa Torres, *Continuous Discovery Habits* - outcomes, opportunities, interviews, assumptions, and continuous product discovery.
2. Jeff Patton, *User Story Mapping* - journeys, end-to-end scope, prioritization, and incremental release slices.
3. Alistair Cockburn, *Writing Effective Use Cases* - actor goals, scenarios, alternate flows, and interaction requirements.
4. Karl Wiegers and Joy Beatty, *Software Requirements*, 3rd ed. - practical requirements elicitation, analysis, specification, validation, prioritization, and management.
5. Ellen Gottesdiener and Mary Gorman, *Discover to Deliver: Agile Product Planning and Analysis* - an early practitioner reference for collaborative product analysis, structured conversations, and seven product dimensions. Useful as a workshop toolkit, but dated and not a complete treatment of modern product discovery or the full lifecycle..
6. Vlad Khononov, *Learning Domain-Driven Design* - domain knowledge, language, subdomains, boundaries, and strategic design.
7. Len Bass, Paul Clements, and Rick Kazman, *Software Architecture in Practice*, 4th ed. - quality attributes, scenarios, architecture decisions, and evaluation.
8. Eric Ries, *The Lean Startup* - assumptions, validated learning, minimum experiments, and Build-Measure-Learn.
9. Ron Kohavi, Diane Tang, and Ya Xu, *Trustworthy Online Controlled Experiments* - outcome metrics, guardrails, experiment design, and trustworthy interpretation.
10. Betsy Beyer, Chris Jones, Jennifer Petoff, and Niall Richard Murphy, eds., *Site Reliability Engineering* - monitoring, releases, incidents, reliability, and operational learning; the book is also [available online](https://sre.google/sre-book/table-of-contents/).

### Bottom line

The lifecycle is neither an arbitrary guess nor an independently validated, named methodology. It is an evidence-informed integration of reputable traditions. Its references justify the component practices; teams still need to validate and tailor the composition for their product, risk, regulatory setting, organization, and delivery environment.
